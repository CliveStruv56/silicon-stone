# Policy Stress-Test

## A practical regulatory-friction diagnostic for companies operating across the US and EU

The Policy Stress-Test helps organisations compare selected US and EU regulatory requirements against their industry context, company size, and operating exposure.

It is designed for SMEs, technology companies, industrial operators, compliance teams, policy teams, procurement leads, product leaders, and executives who need to understand regulatory friction before it becomes a product, market-access, customer, or supplier problem.

The tool does not replace legal advice or a full regulatory audit. Its purpose is to create a defensible first-pass policy exposure record: a structured view of the relevant regimes, likely compliance friction, cross-jurisdiction pressure points, evidence to gather, and actions to prioritise next.

## The Problem It Solves

Many companies now operate in a policy environment where regulatory obligations do not sit neatly inside one jurisdiction.

An AI product may face EU AI Act obligations while depending on US-controlled advanced compute. A connected product may face EU data-access obligations while using cloud infrastructure, suppliers, or customers affected by US export-control or national-security rules. A semiconductor investment may be attractive under both EU and US subsidy regimes but constrained by guardrails, reporting duties, and China-related restrictions.

This creates several practical problems:

- The organisation may not know which EU and US regimes are relevant to its business model.
- It may treat each regulation as a separate legal topic rather than a combined operating constraint.
- It may not know whether friction is low-level monitoring, a compliance workstream, or a board-level issue.
- It may not know what evidence to gather before paying for external legal or technical work.
- It may lack a clear way to compare EU obligations with US constraints.
- It may struggle to prioritise action across legal, product, procurement, security, and operations teams.
- It may not have a concise explanation of regulatory exposure for leadership or customer assurance.

The Policy Stress-Test is built to turn that uncertainty into a structured friction view and a clear next-action list.

## What Makes It Different From A Generic Lead Magnet

Many regulatory tools provide a short checklist or a generic risk score. The Policy Stress-Test is designed to connect policy divergence to operating decisions.

### 1. Cross-Jurisdiction Comparison

The tool is built around the reality that companies often face overlapping EU and US pressures.

It compares policy pairs such as:

- EU AI Act and US export controls
- GDPR and US export controls
- EU Chips Act and US CHIPS Act
- EU Data Act and US export or subsidy constraints

This matters because the business issue is rarely one regulation in isolation. The practical question is how obligations interact across product design, data flows, procurement, customer screening, technical documentation, cloud architecture, and supplier strategy.

### 2. Regulatory Friction, Not Abstract Compliance

The tool uses a friction model rather than simply saying whether a regulation exists.

It helps users distinguish between:

- low monitoring burden
- manageable compliance work
- material operational friction
- high-friction exposure requiring executive visibility

This is more useful for business planning than a generic statement that a regulation "may apply".

### 3. Company-Size Context

The tool asks whether the organisation is a startup, SME, or enterprise.

The result adjusts the framing of the output:

- startups need a narrow first-pass owner and evidence record
- SMEs need named legal and operational ownership
- enterprises need cross-functional governance across legal, product, procurement, security, and regional teams

This matters because the same regulatory exposure creates different execution risk depending on organisational capacity.

### 4. Pressure-Point Analysis

The tool explains the real business tension behind the selected policy pair.

Examples include:

- AI rollout meets compute controls
- data governance meets national-security controls
- subsidy upside with guardrail risk
- data access becomes an operating obligation
- parallel compliance workstreams

This gives the user a sentence they can take into an internal meeting. It turns policy complexity into an operating question.

### 5. Evidence Checklist Built In

The tool identifies what the organisation should gather before spending money on deeper legal, advisory, or technical work.

Examples include:

- product and service inventory by market
- AI system classification evidence
- technical documentation gaps
- customer, supplier, and restricted-country exposure
- data-flow and data-access records
- named owner for each high-friction requirement

This is immediate practical value because many companies know they have policy exposure but do not know what evidence to assemble first.

### 6. Source-Backed Policy Cards

Each policy card can include an official-source link and last-reviewed marker.

This supports trust and makes the tool more credible than an unsupported checklist. It also helps users understand that the tool is a first-pass diagnostic grounded in current policy sources, not a substitute for jurisdiction-specific legal advice.

## Example Use Cases

### AI Product Launch

A company preparing an AI-enabled product for the EU needs to understand how EU AI Act documentation, transparency, human oversight, and risk-classification duties interact with US controls on advanced chips, cloud access, China-facing customers, or model training infrastructure.

The tool identifies the likely friction band, shows the pressure point, and gives the team a first evidence checklist before launch planning hardens.

### Connected Product Review

An industrial or automotive company sells connected equipment into the EU. The EU Data Act may create data-access, contract, interoperability, and support obligations. The tool helps the user compare those obligations with US-side constraints around export controls, supply-chain guardrails, or semiconductor dependency.

### Semiconductor Investment Or Supplier Strategy

A semiconductor company is considering incentives under the EU Chips Act, US CHIPS Act, or both. The tool helps frame the practical issue: subsidy upside may come with reporting, eligibility, China guardrail, and customer-strategy constraints.

### Data Governance And Customer Assurance

A SaaS or AI company receives customer questions about GDPR, AI Act readiness, data transfers, restricted-country exposure, or cloud-provider dependencies. The tool creates a concise first-pass summary of the regimes, requirements, and evidence gaps.

### Board Risk Briefing

A leadership team needs to understand whether regulatory divergence is a minor monitoring issue or a strategic operating constraint. The printable decision brief provides a friction score, pressure point, action plan, and evidence checklist.

### Procurement And Vendor Review

A procurement team is assessing a cloud, AI, semiconductor, connected-product, or data-service vendor. The tool helps identify what questions to ask before contract renewal or deeper dependency.

## Why This Matters

Technology regulation is increasingly operational.

The EU AI Act, EU Data Act, GDPR, DMA, EU Chips Act, US export controls, and US CHIPS guardrails are not just legal texts. They shape product design, data access, compute availability, supplier choices, customer screening, subsidy strategy, documentation, and market access.

For many companies, the hard question is not "what does the regulation say?" but "what does this combination of policies force us to do differently?"

A tool that only summarises regulations is useful for awareness. A tool that connects them to industry context, company size, friction, evidence gaps, and next actions becomes commercially useful.

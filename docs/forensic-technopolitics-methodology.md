# Forensic Technopolitics

**The Silicon and Stone analytical framework**
v1 · May 2026 · Clive Struver, Sanday, Orkney

---

## Why this paper exists

Most technology analysis works backwards from conclusions. The analyst starts with a narrative — "AI will transform everything," "Europe is falling behind," "supply chains are resilient" — and finds evidence to support it. The result is comforting commentary that explains the world after it has already moved.

Forensic Technopolitics works the other way round. It starts with physical facts: where materials come from, how factories actually operate, what regulations actually say, how decisions actually get made. Then it follows the evidence to conclusions, even uncomfortable ones.

This paper sets out the framework Silicon and Stone uses on every published piece. It is short on purpose. The point is to make the method legible — to readers, to the team, and to the reader's own scepticism — not to dress it up.

Read time: ~30 minutes.

---

## 01 · The premise

Technology power in 2026 is no longer a single story. It is the simultaneous compression of three different stories that used to unfold one at a time:

- A **physical-industrial** story about who controls the chips, the foundries, the rare materials, the lithography machines, and the cooling water for the AI hyperscalers.
- A **regulatory** story about the EU AI Act, the US CHIPS Act, export controls, antitrust enforcement, the Donroe Doctrine, and the slow widening of the Atlantic Drift.
- A **human-capability** story about where engineers are moving, which regions are accumulating skill density, and which are hollowing out.

Each of these would have been a defining decade-long story in the 1990s or 2000s. They are now happening simultaneously, with each one feeding back into the other two.

Most coverage handles one strand and treats the other two as background. That is the gap the framework is built to close.

The premise of Forensic Technopolitics is that you cannot understand any single 2026 technology event without holding all three stories in view at once, and that doing so requires a method, not an opinion.

## 02 · Why "Forensic"

The word is deliberate.

A forensic investigator does not start with a theory of the case. They start with the physical evidence and follow it. They photograph the room before they touch anything. They document the chain of custody. They report the negative findings as well as the positive ones. They tell the court what the evidence supports and what it doesn't, and they label their inferences as such.

That posture — slow, evidence-led, transparent about what is and isn't established — is what the framework borrows. Applied to technology policy, it produces fewer confident predictions than mainstream commentary. It catches more of the things that matter: the single points of failure, the impossible compliance positions, the scenarios everyone assumes away.

The contrast is with two other postures common in the field:

- **Techno-optimism** — the Silicon Valley default. Treats every announcement as inevitable, every regulation as friction, every adverse finding as a temporary obstacle.
- **Regulatory pessimism** — the Brussels default. Treats every technology shift as a threat to be managed, every market move as a sovereignty violation.

Forensic Technopolitics rejects both. It calls this posture **Digital Realism** — the conviction that while code moves at the speed of light, human values, laws, and the physical earth are as unmoving as stone, and that good analysis must hold both timescales at once.

## 03 · The framework

Three forensic domains × two analytical methods = a 3 × 2 matrix of six analytical moves. Every Silicon and Stone briefing applies between two and four of them. Every Deep Dive is eligible to apply all six, but the audit only claims the cells the body visibly uses.

### The three domains

#### 1. Supply Chain Forensics — the physical layer

Where are the chokepoints? Who controls them? What does substitution actually cost?

**The process.** Trace the journey of a single component — from a neon supplier in Ukraine to a lithography machine in the Netherlands to a foundry in Taiwan to a packaging house in Penang to a Tier-1 automotive supplier in Stuttgart. Map the 300+ steps. Identify the single points of failure, the geographic concentration risks, the capacity ramps that are on track and the ones that are vapour. Track the human-capital bottlenecks that are usually invisible until something breaks.

**The goal.** Identify the Day Zero failure points for European industry before they appear on a news ticker.

**The questions we ask.** Where does the neon for chip lithography actually come from? What happens when a single Taiwanese fab goes offline? Which European fab investments are on track, and which are vapour?

#### 2. Policy Stress-Testing — the regulatory layer

What does this rule actually do when enforced? Who complies, who pays, who evades?

**The process.** Take a single technological advancement (say, agentic AI) and run it through two operating systems: the US Innovation-First model and the EU Rights-Driven model. Identify the points where compliance with one becomes non-compliance with the other. Track enforcement patterns and regulatory drift. Anticipate second-order effects.

**The goal.** Predict where regulation will stifle growth and where innovation will violate sovereignty, providing a usable roadmap for transatlantic compliance.

**The questions we ask.** Can you comply with both the AI Act and US export controls? What does "adequate" data protection actually mean after Schrems II? How do CHIPS Act and EU Chips Act subsidies interact?

#### 3. Talent & Capability Flow — the human layer

Where are the people moving? Which capabilities are accumulating in a region, and which are hollowing out?

**The process.** Track the flow of senior engineers, fab technicians, AI researchers, regulatory experts, and industrial-policy operators. Map skill density by region, not by national headline. Identify the second-order effects of where the people go: what gets built where they land, what stops being built where they leave.

**The goal.** Connect the macro story to individual professional reality. This is the most underserved domain in existing technology-policy commentary, and the one most likely to determine whether European industrial policy actually produces an industrial base.

**The questions we ask.** Where are TSMC's senior process engineers being recruited from for the German fab? Which European universities are producing AI safety researchers, and where are they ending up? What is the half-life of the regulatory expertise concentrated in Brussels?

### The two methods

Applied to all three domains.

#### A. Scenario-Based Modelling

Instead of a single confident forecast, every analysis runs three scenarios — Low / Medium / High friction — for the same event. The reader gets a structured menu of preparation moves rather than a guess at the future.

We use **Value at Stake** methodology to quantify the impact: how many billions of euros are at risk for a specific industrial sector under each scenario, and what are the trigger events that would shift the system from one scenario to the next?

The output is not a prediction. It is a stress test. The point is to make the operating environment legible enough that a decision-maker can prepare for more than one version of it.

#### B. Long-Memory Filter (the 30-Year Cycle Benchmark)

Pattern-matching the current event against the last thirty years of industrial cycles. The 1986 US-Japan Semiconductor Agreement. The 1990s offshoring wave. The early-2000s globalisation consensus. The 2008 financial crisis and its impact on capital allocation in tech. The 2018 trade-war pivot.

History rhymes; the framework formalises that pattern recognition. If we have seen a pattern fail three times in thirty years, we tell you why the fourth time is no different — and where the genuine novelty is, when there is one.

This is also the lens that separates **Silicon Hype** (temporary noise, indistinguishable from cycles past) from **Stone Truth** (structural change that the prior cycles did not produce).

The 3 × 2 grid of analytical moves. In Sanity Studio you'll see these as the six values in the **Methodology Audit** field on every article.

## 04 · The Methodology Audit

Every published piece carries a **Methodology Audit** — a visible checklist showing which cells of the 3×2 matrix were actually applied in that piece. It looks like a small badge or grid at the top of the article, the start of the YouTube script, and on the briefing cards.

The Audit is the framework's commitment to intellectual honesty. It tells the reader two things:

1. **What we did.** Which analytical moves we ran on this question.
2. **What we didn't do.** Which moves were not applied — usually because the available evidence didn't support them, or because the piece is a Pulse-tier signal where applying all six would be overkill.

A reader who knows the framework can spot when a piece is over-claiming (an Audit that lists more than the body actually delivers) or under-claiming (an Audit that omits a move the body clearly used).

A piece that lists only one cell isn't a failure. It's a Pulse signal. A piece that lists all six is a Deep Dive Audit-tier briefing only if the body visibly applies all six analytical moves. Most Tuesday Stone Briefings sit between two and four cells. The cadence is deliberate.

## 05 · The Stone Truth

Every analysis ends in a single declarative one-line verdict — the **Stone Truth**.

It is the most quotable, citable line in the piece. It is what a reader takes away and what AI search engines will start citing when users ask about European tech sovereignty or chip policy. On the website it appears in italics on every briefing card, distinct from the body.

A worked example, drawn from the Pax Silica analysis:

> "Pax Silica is the 1986 Semiconductor Agreement reborn — a managed-trade pact dressed as an alliance. Europe sat outside that one for fifteen years and emerged with a hollowed manufacturing industry."

Notice what it does:

- It compresses a Long-Memory Filter call (the 1986 reference) and a Scenario-Based outcome ("hollowed manufacturing industry") into a single sentence.
- It is opinionated where the analysis warrants and hedged where it does not. The Pack does not say "Europe will be hollowed again." It says Europe was hollowed last time and the structural setup of the new pact is the same. The reader does the inference.
- It is short enough to be quoted and specific enough to be falsifiable.

A Stone Truth that is too long is a paragraph. A Stone Truth that is too vague is a slogan. The discipline is to land between them.

## 06 · A worked example: Pax Silica

In February 2026 the United States, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and India announced a coordinated semiconductor framework. Public commentary called it variously a "tech alliance," a "supply-chain resilience pact," and (in the more breathless coverage) "the silicon NATO." Europe was not a member.

Apply the framework:

### Supply Chain × Long-Memory Filter

The 1986 US-Japan Semiconductor Trade Agreement set quotas, established price floors, and managed market share between the US and Japan. It was sold publicly as a fairness mechanism. In practice it formalised a managed trade structure that lasted into the late 1990s and shaped where capital and capability were allowed to land.

The Pax Silica announcement reads, structurally, as a multi-party version of the 1986 agreement. The same logic — managed access, coordinated investment, shared technology controls — extended from a bilateral to a five-party arrangement.

### Policy × Scenario Modelling

Three scenarios for European industry under Pax Silica:

- **Low friction**. Europe negotiates side agreements with Japan and South Korea. ASML's Dutch lithography exports continue largely unimpeded. ESMC and STMicro maintain access to advanced packaging in Asia. Cost: regulatory complexity, narrow margin compression. Value at Stake: low single-digit billions.
- **Medium friction**. Pax Silica members coordinate export controls in ways that disadvantage non-members. ASML faces tighter restrictions on EUV exports outside the bloc. European fabs face delays on advanced equipment. Cost: 2–3 year setback on the European fab buildout. Value at Stake: €30–50bn through 2030.
- **High friction**. Pax Silica becomes the de facto regulatory standard for advanced semiconductors. Europe is forced into a junior partner role or excluded entirely. ASML's exports face structural constraint. The German automotive sector, dependent on advanced chips, is exposed. Cost: structural. Value at Stake: €100bn+ through 2030, plus secondary effects on automotive and industrial competitiveness.

### Talent × Scenario Modelling

The pact's coordination mechanisms include skilled-worker visa preferences between member states. Under Medium and High friction scenarios, this accelerates the existing flow of senior process engineers from European fabs to US, Korean, and Taiwanese facilities. The capability that Europe spent the 2020s rebuilding leaves with the people. The fabs may end up built; the skill density to operate them at yield may not.

### Stone Truth

> "Pax Silica is the 1986 Semiconductor Agreement reborn — a managed-trade pact dressed as an alliance. Europe sat outside that one for fifteen years and emerged with a hollowed manufacturing industry."

### Methodology Audit (this piece)

✓ Supply Chain × Long-Memory Filter
✓ Policy × Scenario Modelling
✓ Talent × Scenario Modelling
☐ Supply Chain × Scenario Modelling — *not applied; the supply chain analysis here is historical, not forward-modelled*
☐ Policy × Long-Memory Filter — *not applied; covered in the parallel Atlantic Drift piece*
☐ Talent × Long-Memory Filter — *not applied; would require its own Deep Dive*

That's a four-cell briefing. A full Deep Dive on Pax Silica would close out the remaining cells.

## 07 · Tier and depth

Not every piece applies the full matrix. The framework recognises three tiers, all visible on the briefing cards:

- **Pulse** — 30-second scan. Usually one or two cells. For breaking signals where speed matters and the analytical depth is constrained by what is actually known yet.
- **Briefing** — 5-minute read. Two to four cells. The default Stone Briefing tier. The Tuesday Stone Briefing on the YouTube channel maps to this.
- **Audit** — Deep dive, 20+ minute read. Eligible for all six cells, but evidence-led in what it claims. Foundational pieces. The format reserved for the questions that will compound — Pax Silica, the AI Act enforcement cliff, the German automotive supply-chain exposure.

The tier is set in Sanity Studio on every article. The Methodology Audit on the published piece reflects what was actually applied — which should match the tier, but doesn't always, and the Audit is always the source of truth.

## 08 · What the framework is not

The framework is a method, not a worldview. It does not predetermine the conclusion of any piece.

It is also not:

- **A forecasting service.** We do not produce single confident predictions. We produce stress tests that hold up across scenarios.
- **An alarmist channel.** The Long-Memory Filter is as likely to identify "this is a 1990s-style cycle that will resolve itself" as it is to identify a structural break. Both are useful.
- **A position paper.** We do not advocate. We trace evidence. The reader makes the call.
- **Captured.** No vendor money, no government commission, no industry body sets the topics. VBP Solutions (Clive's separate consultancy) takes commercial engagements; Silicon and Stone does not.

This last point is structurally important. The framework only does its job if the conclusions can fall where the evidence lands, even when that's awkward.

## 09 · How it shows up on the platform

The framework is not abstract. It is built into the CMS:

- Every article carries the **Methodology Audit** field — the six-cell checklist.
- Every article carries the **Stone Truth** field — the one-line verdict.
- Every article carries the **Intelligence Tier** (Pulse / Briefing / Audit).
- Every article carries one or more **Personas** — the decision-makers the piece is for.
- Every article is **indexed in Pinecone** on publish, so future pieces can retrieve and cite the back-catalogue automatically.
- The four interactive **tools** on the website (Compliance Checker, Supply Chain Mapper, Scenario Modeler, Policy Stress-Test) are operational expressions of the framework — they let readers run a single question through one of the analytical methods themselves.

The framework, in other words, is not just the editorial discipline. It is the schema. Every drop of content the platform produces is structurally legible against it.

## 10 · For Jane

If you've read this far: you don't need to apply Forensic Technopolitics yourself. Clive does that. What you need to do is **recognise the structure when you see it** so that:

- When you polish a draft in Sanity, you can spot a piece whose Methodology Audit doesn't match what the body actually delivered, and flag it.
- When you tag a piece for personas, you can see which analytical moves the piece used and infer which persona is most likely to act on it.
- When you write social copy or newsletter intros for Hyperagent's drafts, you can tell whether the piece is a Pulse, Briefing, or Audit and pitch accordingly.
- When something off-brand appears (a Stone Truth that's a slogan, an Audit that over-claims, a piece that drifts into commentary), you can use your veto right (see Welcome Pack §04).

That's it. The framework is the spine. Your job is to keep the spine straight as the body of work grows.

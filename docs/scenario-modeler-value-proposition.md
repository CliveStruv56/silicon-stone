# Scenario Modeler

## A practical geopolitical scenario diagnostic for technology value chains

The Scenario Modeler helps organisations understand how geopolitical futures could affect technology value chains, sector exposure, regulatory friction, and operational resilience.

It is designed for SMEs, industrial operators, technology leaders, policy teams, procurement teams, investors, and board-level decision-makers who need to think beyond single-point forecasts but do not have a dedicated geopolitical risk function.

The tool does not claim to predict the future. Its purpose is to create a defensible first-pass scenario record: a structured view of plausible shocks, sector-level value at stake, cascade effects, early-warning indicators, and practical mitigation moves.

## The Problem It Solves

Most organisations know that technology supply chains are exposed to geopolitics, but they often struggle to translate that concern into decisions.

This creates several practical problems:

- The organisation may not know which geopolitical scenarios are most relevant to its operating model.
- It may treat geopolitical risk as a news topic rather than a planning input.
- It may focus on one familiar disruption while missing second-order effects across chips, cloud, AI, regulation, data, and procurement.
- It may not know which early-warning indicators to monitor.
- It may lack a clear way to compare low-friction, medium-friction, and high-friction futures.
- It may not know what practical mitigation moves to take before a shock occurs.
- It may struggle to explain geopolitical exposure in board-ready commercial terms.

The modeler is built to turn that uncertainty into a structured scenario view and a clear next-action list.

## What Makes It Different From A Generic Lead Magnet

Many scenario tools present broad narratives with little operational value. This modeler is designed to connect geopolitical futures to technology value chains and practical decisions.

### 1. Technology Value-Chain Focus

The tool is structured around the specific dependencies that matter for modern technology operations.

It distinguishes between:

- advanced logic
- advanced packaging
- HBM and AI accelerator constraints
- cloud and AI compute
- export-controlled technology
- connected products
- data services
- regulated AI
- EU and US regulatory divergence

This matters because a geopolitical shock rarely affects only one layer. A Taiwan scenario, for example, is not only a foundry problem. It can also become a packaging, memory, logistics, insurance, procurement, and government-allocation problem.

### 2. Scenario-Based Drift Modelling

The modeler reflects the Silicon and Stone scenario methodology: futures that compound rather than simple linear forecasts.

Current scenarios include:

- Taiwan Strait Advanced Logic & Packaging Shock
- US-China Controls & Retaliation Spiral
- EU Strategic Autonomy Execution Gap
- AI Act Enforcement & Digital Simplification
- Atlantic Tech Friction & Dual Compliance Drift

This helps users move from vague geopolitical concern to specific planning questions.

### 3. Exposure Lens Built In

The tool asks for operating context rather than showing every user the same output.

It adjusts exposure based on:

- sector
- operating geography
- dependency type
- sourcing flexibility

This makes the result more relevant for an industrial manufacturer, AI infrastructure buyer, financial services firm, healthcare operator, or connected-product vendor.

### 4. Cascade Effects And Board Briefs

The modeler shows how the initial shock propagates.

Each scenario includes:

- primary impact

## Why This Matters

Technology value chains are now shaped by geopolitics, not just engineering and market demand.

Advanced chips, AI infrastructure, cloud services, data transfers, export controls, industrial automation, connected products, and AI regulation are increasingly affected by government decisions. A business may not think of itself as geopolitical, but it can still be exposed through suppliers, cloud providers, product architecture, procurement rules, customer contracts, or regulatory obligations.

A tool that only describes possible futures is useful for awareness. A tool that connects those futures to the user's operating context, exposure, warning signals, and mitigation moves becomes commercially useful.
